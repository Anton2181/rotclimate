## 2. The winter is a moisture error, not a wind error

Winter (December–February) over land. The table compares the model's
column water with NCEP's precipitable water; the winds are r41's own.

| region | model column water | NCEP | rain × observed | rain × with NCEP winds |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Manchuria | 4.8 mm | 3.2 | 2.2 | 3.3 |
| Korea | 11.7 | 6.4 | 2.4 | 2.3 |
| Japan | 14.4 | 8.3 | 1.9 | 1.8 |
| North China Plain | 9.6 | 5.9 | 3.2 | 3.7 |
| East China | 18.0 | 14.5 | 1.1 | 1.5 |
| eastern Europe | 6.8 | 8.9 | 1.2 | 0.8 |
| US Midwest | 9.7 | 9.0 | 1.2 | 1.6 |

**What the table shows:**
- **East Asia's winter columns are too moist,** by 1.5–1.8 times NCEP.
  Europe's and the US Midwest's are about right.
- **The winds are not the cause.** Observed winds do not dry the columns;
  Manchuria and the North China Plain get wetter still.

**This was found once before.** The r39 report (`PHYSICS_GAPS_20261030.md`
§11) traced the moisture to the sea column under cold continental air.
- **e58** holds that column at the air's temperature. It dries East
  Asia's winters strongly at 0.5–1.0.
- **But it costs annual rain** in Japan, South Brazil and the British
  Isles, which are short of summer rain. So r41 carries it at only 0.18.

The winter cannot be fixed until the warm-season frontal rain is.

## 3. e68h: storms follow only humid baroclinic zones (opt-in, not adopted)

**The switch:** `storm_baroclinic_humid_rh` x counts e68's north–south
contrast only where the smoothed column is humid, rising over 0.2 above
relative humidity x. The Meiyu–Baiu front is a sharp moisture front with
a weak temperature step. A summer desert's hot edge is a dry contrast,
and in the ungated e68 it pulled the storms into Mexico, the Maghreb,
Iran, the Cape and eastern Australia.

It is off by default, and r41 reproduces bit for bit.

**Screen** (dev31, paired with a fresh r41 run on the same code):

| e68 k 1.0 with | S | training | holdout | mid-latitude | dev12 | rain loss |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| humid 0.45 | 0.9981 | 0.993 | 1.010 | 1.031 | 0.985 | −0.5 % |
| humid 0.60 | **0.9930** | 0.984 | 1.009 | 1.029 | 0.979 | −1.5 % |

**Not adopted.** Both settings worsen the holdout and mid-latitude groups.

**What it does:**
- **The humid gate keeps the storms off the desert edges,** as intended.
  Mexico, Iran and the Cape no longer worsen.
- **It also leaves East Asia where it was.** Japan, Korea and East China
  stay within 0.5 % of r41.
- **The storms move instead to the winter-rain coasts:**
  - better rain in Iberia (0.84–0.86 of its loss), the Great Basin
    (0.85–0.87), the British Isles (0.85–0.87) and California (0.88–0.91);
  - worse in New Zealand (1.17), the US Midwest (up to 1.17), western
    Europe (1.07) and the Alps (1.09–1.10).

So gating by humidity behaves like the earlier gating by cool air (e68,
cold 10 °C). Both remove the damage at the desert edges, and both remove
the East Asian gain with it.

**Where East Asia's spring storms belong:**
- **The rule they need is not "the strongest contrast".** On each meridian
  through East Asia, the humid strong-contrast zone in spring lies over the
  sea and further north (the Kuril and Okhotsk front). The centroid follows
  it there.
- **The Meiyu belt is held at 30–35° N by the jet's split around the
  Tibetan Plateau.** That is a stationary-wave effect of the plateau, which
  the model's zonal storm band cannot represent.

**The next design** is a downstream-of-plateau storm term. Downstream of a
high plateau in the cold half of the year, the storm band runs along the
plateau's equatorward edge, carried east by the westerlies. The rule is
general enough for the fantasy map, if it has a plateau. It needs its own
frozen screen.

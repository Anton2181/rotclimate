# Sharpen the swamp, then stress-test the spin

The best next change to rotclimate comes from your own legend, not from the climate literature: **require the swamp to be hot on average**. Add a soft rule of mean annual temperature at or above about 21 °C. Tested on round 14's parameters with no recalibration, it lifts the F1 objective from **0.427 to 0.444**, and painted land in its own zone from **53.5% to 57.4%**. It does this mostly by nearly doubling swamp precision (0.26 → 0.45). The wetland literature points instead at drainage terms: a wetness index, height above the nearest river, upstream area. Those do not separate swamp from the warm-wet coast on this map, because four flat height tiers leave them no relief to measure. The second priority is the spin verdict. I re-scored both spins' best fits on 12 surrounding worlds the calibration never saw. Reversed spin's objective lead shrank from 0.006 to **0.004, with a 95% interval that includes zero**. Its lead in painted land correct held on **all 12 worlds (+4.5 points)**. So the lean toward reversed spin is real in the *pattern*: wet west coast, dry south-east, Mediterranean east coast. That pattern is what the one full climate-model study of reversed spin predicts. The *headline score* cannot yet tell the spins apart. A fair rematch costs an evening to a night on 4 cores. The literature mostly confirms the model's design. Its rain shadow is as steep as real ones, and the big published physics schemes would cost a lot and move this map little. Two physics gaps deserve attention. Summers in the painted swamp peak 3–4 °C hotter than on the Gulf Coast, and hotter than almost every equally wet city on Earth. The subtropical highs also never strengthen in summer. A full 3-D climate model, ExoPlaSim, run on the east–west mirrored map, can check the *direction* of the spin result in about a day of computing. It cannot judge a gap of 0.006.

## Heat, not drainage, separates the painted swamp

The wetland literature has a clear recipe. Large wetlands form where a climatic water surplus meets converging drainage near base level. Tootchi and colleagues map them with the index TCI = ln(a·Pe/tanβ). Here *a* is the upstream drainage area, *Pe* the summed monthly surplus of rain over evaporation, and *tanβ* the slope. They label the top 6–15% of land by that index as wetland ([Tootchi et al. 2019](https://essd.copernicus.org/articles/11/189/2019/)). Height above the nearest drainage (HAND) below about **5.9 m**, on a 5 m terrain model, marks waterlogged ground in a calibrated catchment study ([Gharari et al. 2011](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/15/3275/2011/)). Fan and Miguez-Macho argue that lowland wetlands are fed from *below*, where groundwater cannot drain to the sea ([Fan & Miguez-Macho 2011](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00382-010-0829-8)). Even Azgaar's map generator adds river flow to its wetland rule ([Azgaar FMG](https://github.com/Azgaar/Fantasy-Map-Generator/blob/master/src/generators/biomes-generator.ts)). The research notes therefore expected drainage, not climate, to split the painted swamp from the flat, rainy warm-wet lowland.

The map says otherwise. A diagnostic on the current best map (14 km grid, round 14 parameters) tested each feature on the 1,606 cells where the swamp climate rule already holds. Most are painted swamp (459), warm-wet (599), tree (172) or Mediterranean (108), and 259 are unpainted. The score used is AUC: how well one number sorts two groups, where 0.5 is a coin flip and 1 (or 0) is a perfect split.

| feature | AUC, painted swamp vs other painted zones | what it means |
|---|---|---|
| upstream drainage area | 0.48 | no separation |
| wetness index weighted by surplus (Tootchi-style) | 0.43 | none, slightly backwards |
| HAND to the model's rivers | 0.69, **backwards** | swamp sits *higher* above channels (median 2.0 m vs 0.0 m) |
| HAND to your drawn rivers / drawn-river density | 0.50 / 0.54 | none |
| distance to sea / slope | 0.53 / 0.46 | none |
| annual rain; rain-to-dryness ratio | swamp drier: 1,840 vs 2,330 mm; AUC 0.29 | **backwards** |
| **mean annual temperature** | **0.96** (22.3 vs 21.0 °C) | near-perfect |
| hottest month | 0.84 (32.3 vs 30.1 °C) | strong |
| coldest month | 0.59 (8.9 vs 8.6 °C) | weak |

Drainage fails here for a mechanical reason. The terrain is four flat tiers, so the lowland has no micro-relief for HAND or a wetness index to read. These indices were built on 5–30 m terrain models, and their values depend on pixel size ([Tootchi et al. 2019](https://essd.copernicus.org/articles/11/189/2019/)). This matches round 12's floodplain test, where painted swamp was no more river-fed than other lowland (0.21 vs 0.21). Wetness fails because **the painted swamp is drier** than its look-alikes, with a surplus of 710 vs 1,230 mm. Any wetness-weighted index favours the warm-wet coast. Heat is what the painter seems to have meant, and the legend says so: "Hot and wet, swampy" against "Warm and wet". This also revises the README's view that "no rule should split them". The spill area is wetter but cooler, and a heat rule splits them cleanly.

I tested three versions of a heat requirement on round 14's three worlds. Each multiplies the existing swamp membership by a soft step. The calibration settings reproduce both spins' scores exactly (0.4271 and 0.4210).

| swamp rule (static, no recalibration) | reversed objective | reversed correct | swamp precision / coverage / F1 | Earth-like objective |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| current rule | 0.427 | 53.5% | 0.26 / 0.34 / 0.30 | 0.421 |
| + mean annual ≥ ~21 °C | **0.444** | **57.4%** | **0.45** / 0.30 / **0.36** | 0.429 |
| + mean annual ≥ ~21.5 °C | 0.440 | 58.6% | 0.51 / 0.26 / 0.34 | 0.425 |
| + hottest month ≥ ~31 °C | 0.419 | 57.8% | 0.35 / 0.23 / 0.27 | 0.430 |

**Use annual mean, not hottest month.** The hottest-month form scores worse on the map you favour, and it would also make the swamp require an unearthly climate. In the project's own WorldClim reference set there are 1,724 cities with at least 1,500 mm of rain and a coldest month of 4 °C or warmer. **Only 1.6% have a hottest month of 31 °C, and 0.2% reach 32 °C.** The Gulf Coast bayou country that the README cites peaks at 28–29 °C (New Orleans 28.3, Houston 28.8, Baton Rouge 27.9). The annual-mean form sits in Earth's range. A 21 °C cut puts Houston (21.0 °C) on the soft edge, New Orleans (20.7) just below it, and Miami, Tampa and Guangzhou (22–25 °C) inside. In this static test the gain comes from fewer false swamps on the warm-wet coast. The share of painted swamp named swamp dips (38% → 36%), which a recalibration may win back. Two honesty points. First, I picked the annual-mean form on the legend and on Earth plausibility *before* seeing the spin results. But the form matters more than the spin gap: annual mean widens reversed spin's static lead to 0.015, and hottest month flips it to −0.010. So fix the rule's wording on its merits, write it into the README's rule table, and only then rerun both spins.

## The spin lead lives in the zones, not the headline score

The physics behind the reversed-spin reading is sound. The equations of motion are unchanged if you flip the spin, mirror the map east–west, and flip the east–west winds. The only leftover is the direction the sun crosses the sky. In a 6,990-year Earth-system run that flipped only the spin and kept the sun's path, that leftover was "for the most part negligible" ([Mikolajewicz et al. 2018](https://esd.copernicus.org/articles/9/1191/2018/esd-9-1191-2018.pdf)). The same study found that Köppen zones mirror "to a first approximation", that global temperature changed by only **0.14 K**, and that "deserts shift from the western subtropical continental boundaries to the southeast". A visual reading of its coarse Köppen map is approximate, but it shows the south-east US as desert, the New England coast as dry-summer and coastal California as fully humid ([Mikolajewicz et al. 2018](https://esd.copernicus.org/articles/9/1191/2018/)). That is your painted pattern. Mediterranean climates sit under the sinking air on one flank of the summer subtropical high ([Seager et al. 2019](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10112964)). Under reversed spin that flank faces your east coast. A second climate model found wind *speeds* similar under either spin ([Kamphuis et al. 2011](https://cp.copernicus.org/articles/7/487/2011/)). rotclimate already flips every east–west term the literature names: the mean wind, the turning of thermal winds, the current side, the subsidence side and the storm-track side. Its planet-wide energy balance rightly ignores spin.

The headline number is weaker than the physics. Calibration scores every candidate on the same three worlds, seeds 1–3. Those worlds are drawn on a fixed grid keyed only by the seed, so same-seed worlds keep the same coastline shapes. That is "common random numbers": it keeps candidate comparisons fair within a run ([Management Science 1999](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.45.11.1570)). But the winner is the best of ~300 tries on those three coastlines. I re-scored both finalists on 12 new worlds (seeds 101–112):

| | reversed spin | Earth-like spin | gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| calibration worlds 1–3 (best of ~300) | 0.427 | 0.421 | +0.006 |
| 12 fresh worlds, F1 objective | 0.423 | 0.420 | **+0.004** (per-world 95% interval −0.007 to +0.014) |
| four separate fresh 3-world sets | 0.413–0.430 | 0.414–0.425 | −0.011 to +0.016 |
| painted land correct, 12 fresh worlds | 52.8% | 48.2% | **+4.5 points, ahead on 12 of 12** |

Two of the four alternative three-world sets would have crowned Earth-like spin. Reversed spin loses about 0.004 to the winner's curse, Earth-like spin about 0.001. The correct-share lead is solid, and it reflects the zones the literature says spin controls: the warm-wet west coast and the hot-dry south-east. Three other things move the headline more than 0.006 does: the wording of the swamp rule (above), the search bounds (next section), and stopping time. Earth-like spin's best came in its very last generation (0.412 → 0.421), so it had not finished.

A defensible rematch follows benchmarking practice ([Bartz-Beielstein et al. 2020](https://d-nb.info/1214641016/34)). First, freeze the rules and bounds. Second, re-score each spin's top 3–5 candidates on the same 50–60 fresh worlds. The per-world difference has a spread of about 0.02, so about 60 worlds are needed to pin the mean within ±0.005. That takes 20–40 minutes. Third, run 3–4 independent searches per spin overnight (7–8 hours for 8 runs) to measure search-to-search spread σ. Runs needed per spin ≈ 15.7σ²/δ², so a 0.006 gap needs 11 runs if σ = 0.005 and 44 if σ = 0.01. Fourth, declare in advance that a gap inside ±0.01 is a tie. If it lands there, the honest README line is: "the painted pattern is the reversed-spin pattern, and the fit score cannot separate the spins." That is still a strong worldbuilding result.

## The physics needs seasonal fixes, not new schemes

Parameters stuck at a bound show where the model strains. In each spin's best fit, 10 of 49 genes sit within 4% of a limit:

| | reversed spin (round 14) | Earth-like spin (round 15p) |
|---|---|---|
| switched off | ocean currents (0.013 of 8 K) | storm-track east–west asymmetry (0.001), convective rain (0.21 of 6) |
| at ceiling | storm-track rain (5.0), sea-air humidity (0.90), upland tier thickness (1,800 m), land beyond the south, south-east and north-east (97–100%) | monsoon strength (2.95 of 3), monsoon scale (894 of 900 km), continental seasonality (1.66 of 1.7), edge continuation (700 km) |
| at floor | mean mid-latitude wind (2.0 m/s), land air adjustment time (0.62 d), lowland top (100 m) | mean mid-latitude wind (2.0 m/s), evaporation time (0.50 d), lowland top (109 m), inland-sea warming (0.23 of 8) |
| close behind | subsidence east–west asymmetry (0.92 of 1.0) | — |

Both spins want the imposed mid-latitude wind as weak as allowed. They lean instead on storm-eddy mixing, which is physically reasonable, because real mid-latitude transport is mostly done by storms. Earth-like spin has pushed its monsoon and continental knobs to their ceilings. It is trying to build, from land–sea heating, the east–west contrast that its own wind belts set up the wrong way round. That fits the spin story, but it also means wider bounds would help Earth-like spin more. Hence item #3: settle the bounds on physical grounds first. Some are true limits (land fraction 1.0, current strength 0). Others are modelling choices (the 3.0 monsoon ceiling, the 2 m/s wind floor, the 5.0 storm-track ceiling).

Ocean currents are off in the reversed fit. Refining them is therefore poor value: no basin-width scaling, no upwelling fog. One abstract-level source holds that the atmosphere, not ocean heat transport, sets most east–west coastal contrast ([Seager et al. 2002](https://ocp.ldeo.columbia.edu/res/div/ocp/gs/pubs/Seager_etal_QJ_2002.pdf), search summary). The missing piece the literature does name is **seasonal strength**. Summer highs are stronger than winter ones, and they sit over one side of each ocean basin ([Nakamura & Miyasaka](https://ams.confex.com/ams/pdfpapers/78520.pdf)). Monsoon heating over a continent drives sinking air beside it ([Rodwell & Hoskins 2001](https://doi.org/10.1175/1520-0442(2001)014%3C3192:SAASM%3E2.0.CO;2)). In `model.py` the sinking belt's latitude moves with the season, but its strength and east–west asymmetry stay the same all year. The reversed fit has pushed that asymmetry to 0.92 of its 1.0 maximum. One gene multiplying both by (1 + a·season) is the smallest literature-backed fix (item #4).

The second gap is **summer heat in wet lowland**. Your painted swamp runs from 8.9 °C to **32.3 °C**, a swing of 23 °C. New Orleans runs from 11.6 to 28.3 °C. In `model.py` land temperature comes from the latitude-band energy balance plus a calibrated offset, and it ignores how wet the ground is. The reversed fit sets that offset to +3.1 K over land and −3.0 K over sea, and its land air locks onto the land value as fast as the bounds allow. On Earth, wet ground spends sunshine on evaporation, which caps summer heat. That is my reading of why so few wet cities pass 31 °C, not a sourced finding. Damping land temperature by the soil wetness the model already tracks is item #9. One untested thought: the painted swamp is drier than the spill area, so wetness damping would cool the spill area more. The heat contrast that item #1 relies on could then survive or even grow.

Rain shadows need no new scheme. Your 1,548 → 347 mm drop over 40 km halves every ~18.5 km. Across the Olympic Mountains rain falls from over 3 m/yr in the Hoh valley to 0.4 m/yr at Sequim ([Anders et al. 2007](https://earthweb.ess.washington.edu/roe/GerardWeb/Publications_files/AndersEtAl_Olympics_JHM07.pdf)), less than 35 miles away ([NPS](https://www.nps.gov/olym/planyourvisit/weather-brochure.htm)). That halves every 19–23 km. The Southern Alps fall from 11–12 m/yr to under 1 m/yr ([Roe 2005](https://earthweb.ess.washington.edu/roe/GerardWeb/Publications_files/Roe_OrogPrec_AnnRev05.pdf)). Your shadow is realistic for a tall, continuous ridge. It is too sharp only if the barrier is hill tier, since simple upslope physics holds for ranges over ~1.5 km high and ~40 km wide (Roe). Smith–Barstad linear theory would not soften it, because it "still predicts extremely dry leeward sides" ([Hergarten & Robl 2022](https://gmd.copernicus.org/articles/15/2063/2022/)). The ready-made Python version also mishandles non-square grids ([fastscape source](https://github.com/fastscape-lem/orographic-precipitation/blob/master/orographic_precipitation/orographic_precipitation.py), bug found in the research session). Falling rain drifts about U·τ ≈ 36 km in the classic Oregon setup ([Smith, Barstad & Bonneau](https://ams.confex.com/ams/pdfpapers/76934.pdf)). That is 1–3 cells, which fits your "no gain at 15–30 km". The 50 km blow-up was most likely numerical. A downwind implicit sweep, as in Hergarten & Robl's LFPM, is stable at any drift length. LFPM is the closest published cousin of rotclimate's moisture scheme. Adding its cloud-water field would buy a physical spillover scale of only 1–2 cells at 14 km.

## A real climate model can check the sign, not the size

The mirror symmetry allows an outside check without retrograde support in the GCM (general circulation model: a full 3-D weather-and-climate simulator). ExoPlaSim has no documented reversed-spin option, and its calendar code resets the year length whenever the rotation period changes ([ExoPlaSim source](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/alphaparrot/ExoPlaSim/master/exoplasim/__init__.py)). So run it with Earth spin on the east–west mirrored map, then mirror the output back. Worldbuilding Pasta takes the same shortcut: "you'd get the same result if you just flipped the topography map horizontally" ([Worldbuilding Pasta](https://worldbuildingpasta.blogspot.com/2023/06/climate-explorations-day-length.html)). The model installs with pip, needs gfortran, accepts a custom land mask and terrain, and splits work across 4 cores. At the coarse T21 grid it ran a model year in **3.75 minutes** on a 2017 quad-core laptop. At T42 it took 15–16 minutes per year on 16 threads ([Paradise et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.07685)), which suggests about an hour per year on 4 cores (extrapolated). One user reported T42 crashes with custom maps ([Worldbuilding Pasta comments](https://worldbuildingpasta.blogspot.com/2021/11/an-apple-pie-from-scratch-part-vi.html)).

The fair design is four runs. Run each spin at its own best fit: latitude, tilt, tier heights, and one realisation of its off-map land, mirrored for reversed spin. Then run each parameter set under the other spin, which separates the effect of spin from the effect of refitting. At T21 that is about a day; at T42 it is about 30 hours per run. Resolution caps what this can tell you. A T21 cell is ~625 km, so your 3,700 × 1,700 km continent is about **6 × 3 cells**, and at T42 about 12 × 6. The test can settle sign-level questions. Which coast is wetter in each latitude band? Where does the subtropical dry belt meet each coast? Does rain peak in summer or winter along each coast? Those are the contrasts behind reversed spin's 12-of-12 correct-share lead. It cannot judge a 0.006 gap, the swamp, rain shadows or 14 km zone edges. Its slab ocean has no currents ([Paradise et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.07685)), which here is a feature, since your reversed fit has switched them off too. Read a "no difference" result cautiously. Worldbuilders report a bias toward Mediterranean climates ([Worldbuilding Pasta](https://worldbuildingpasta.blogspot.com/2021/11/an-apple-pie-from-scratch-part-vi.html)) and subtropical highs merged into one ridge ([Cartographers' Guild](https://www.cartographersguild.com/showthread.php?p=452004), anecdotal). Either could blur exactly the asymmetry in question. Isca is the research-grade alternative, with heavier installation and unconfirmed support for negative rotation ([Vallis et al. 2018](https://gmd.copernicus.org/articles/11/843/2018/)).

## Conclusion

The literature's main gift is confirmation, not new machinery. The mirror reading of reversed spin, the flipped east–west terms and the steep rain shadow all match published physics. The fixes the literature offers for generic problems (drainage wetland indices, linear orographic theory, basin-scaled currents) miss this map's real bottlenecks. The bigger lesson comes from this map's own numbers. Choices outside the physics currently move the headline more than the physics does: the wording of one zone rule, where the search bounds sit, which three random worlds were drawn, and when the search stopped. Lock those down on stated grounds first. Every later physics experiment then becomes easier to judge.

For the story of the world, the spin claim should change shape rather than strength. "Reversed spin scores 0.427 against 0.421" is fragile. "The painting is the textbook reversed-spin pattern, a mirror-image North America, and Earth-like spin can only imitate it by maxing out its monsoon" is robust: it holds on every fresh world, and it matches a full Earth-system model. Separately, the hot-wet summer bias is the one physics flaw a reader would notice in a climograph. It is worth fixing before it gets baked into a swamp threshold.

## Suggested order of work

1. Add the mean-annual heat requirement to the swamp rule (~21 °C, soft), update the rule table, and recalibrate both spins from their current bests (~2 h).
2. Settle the search bounds on physical grounds, the same for both spins.
3. Re-score each spin's top candidates on 50–60 fresh shared worlds (20–40 min), and report the gap with its interval and the correct-share lead.
4. Add the summer-strength gene for subtropical sinking; one run per spin.
5. Run Morris screening and freeze genes that matter for no zone.
6. Run 3–4 independent searches per spin overnight, then re-score them as in step 3.
7. Add wet-ground cooling of land temperature, then re-check the swamp threshold.
8. Optionally, run ExoPlaSim on the mirrored map as an outside check of which coast is wet and which is dry.

# Rainfall at real cities

The question is how close the Earth model comes to the rain of real cities,
one city at a time. `scripts/city_rain.py` takes every GeoNames city of
50,000 or more people (`data/real_cities_worldclim.csv`: WorldClim 2.1
normals, 1970–2000, sampled at the city) that lies within 40 km of a scored
cell centre of the 31 development regions: **3,667 cities**. Each city is
compared with its 56 km cell's simulated monthly rain.

A perfect 56 km model would still miss each city by the rain that varies
inside one cell. The same comparison with the observed cell mean (WorldClim
averaged over the cell) measures that floor.

## Three faults behind the largest errors

**The South Asian monsoon is missing.** North India's map gets 28–31 mm a
month in July and August against 219–224 observed; the worst cities are the
Himalayan foothill towns (Haldwani, Dehradun, Rishikesh, Hetauda: 37–74 mm a
year against 1,500–2,000). The column over the Ganges plain holds 35 mm of
water against a saturation of about 70, so the rain closure, which rises
steeply with column humidity, gives little. Observed sea temperatures add
only 11 mm in July; stronger monsoon winds add nothing. The foothill flow
runs along the range from the east, as the real monsoon-trough easterlies do,
so neither the slope term nor freeing it from the terrain drag lifts it.
Probes that raise the supply (`flux_convergence` 0.5, `ocean_rh` 0.85,
`monsoon_heating` 4) reach 53–96 mm in July and make Mexico, eastern
Australia and the south-east US too wet.

**Cold continental winters are too wet.** Manchuria gets 42 mm in January
against 5, Korea 96 against 29, the North China Plain 33 against 8. The
column holds 5.4 mm against about 3 observed; its saturation over the frozen
ground is about 3.5 mm, so the surplus snows out as supersaturation. The
water arrives by the storm-eddy exchange, a Gaussian of 852 km that carries
Pacific water inland against the winter westerlies.

**Spring storms rain on the deserts.** Iran's arid cells get 43–53 mm a month
in April–June against 1–17 (the e37 storm-width test, rejected: narrowing the
storm track helps Iran and costs the wet regions).

Two more ideas were probed on single regions (not the gate):

- **e38b**: air the eddies move onto colder ground keeps at most its
  relative humidity (`eddy_rh_cap`). Manchuria's January falls 42 → 1 mm
  (5 observed), Iran's loss 0.83 → 0.58; but winter rain that really comes
  from moist air riding over cold ground vanishes: US Midwest January 9 mm
  (43 observed), eastern China 6 (34), Japan 28 (63).
- **e40**: moister columns over seas warmer than 25 °C (`ocean_rh_warm`,
  after the observed rise of precipitable water with sea temperature). At
  0.1, north India's July 28 → 34 mm, and eastern China, the SE US, Japan,
  south Brazil and Texas gain; eastern Australia, already 60 % too wet, goes
  0.55 → 0.77. Its trade-wind air carries 37 mm of water inland and rains
  124 mm a month in summer against 88.

Each knob trades regions, so a joint search of twelve rain knobs, scored as
the gate scores, ran next (`scripts/gate_calibrate.py`,
`calibration/rain_knobs_20261024/plan.json`). **None of its 64 candidates
beat r35.** The best overall score was 0.9985 (0.5 % is needed) with the
former holdout group 2.3 % worse; the next, 0.9989 with it 1.9 % worse. The
present knobs sit at their joint optimum: the next gain has to be structural.

western Europe 0.25 → 0.37 (Atlantic water carried further inland in
winter), Japan, the Midwest and Mexico worse, north India unchanged. A second
joint search with it (`calibration/rain_knobs2_20261024/plan.json`, frozen
before results) is running.

## The sea under north India

North India's lowlands are not only dry. They are 4–5 °C too cold from
October to May (January 11.0 °C against 15.5; May 28.6 against 33.3), and all
of peninsular India with them. The cause is offshore. The model's Arabian Sea
is 15–19 °C in January against 24–26 observed (WOA18), and 21–25 °C in July
against 27–29. Splitting the sea-surface terms at 17 °N, 68 °E:

| Term | January | July |
|---|---|---|
| zonal ocean + offset | 23.8 | 26.5 |
| boundary current | −6.2 | −6.2 |
| enclosure pull | −0.5 | −0.3 |
| observed | 26.5 | 28.3 |

The boundary-current term tells which side of a sea a cell lies on from the
land around it. India lies to the east, so the sea off its west coast is
treated as the cold eastern limb of a gyre, as off California, at the full
−6 °C. A cold west-coast current carries water down from the westerlies,
and the Arabian Sea meets land at 25 °N: it has no gyre and no such current.
The Bay of Bengal, the Red Sea, the Persian Gulf and the Gulf of California
are closed off the same way.

**e42** (`current_gyre`): the cold current needs open sea due poleward. Where
the sea meets land before 30° there is none; where it is open to 40° or
beyond, the full current; linear between, smoothed over the current's scale.
The open reach is measured on WorldClim's land (`earth.sea_reach`); other
grids measure their own. The warm east-coast currents are unchanged, and
the setting adds no fitted number.

Sea-surface score (`scripts/fit_sst.py score`, dev31, no land run):

## The monsoon's water: convergence on the large scale

Of the 3,667 cities, north India's 406 carry 72 % of the squared log error
(log RMSE 1.92, rain 91 % short). Three ideas that did not bring the monsoon:

- **e42**, the warmer Arabian Sea above: +37 mm a year.
- **e43**, cloud shading measured against the globe's land at each latitude
  (`cloud_reference` "earth", the land-mean cloud from WorldClim rain and
  humidity through the model's own cloud formula) instead of the domain's
  own latitude mean. In May at 27 °N clear north India and cloudy south
  China share one domain mean; the real Delhi is 6.5 °C above its latitude's
  land mean and Changsha 5 °C below. At `cloud_radiation` 10 north India's
  spring warms 1–2 °C and east China's cools 2 °C, but the dry-modelled
  monsoon months get 1.6 °C too hot, and Iran, Mexico (rain too high, so too
  cloudy) and the summers of California and Chile go the wrong way. Off.
- A pressure field blind to the heat north of the Himalaya (scratch test):
  the model's July low sits over Mongolia (−10 hPa) and pressure falls
  northward across India, so the plain's wind blows from the Thar. Hiding
  that heat moved July rain 24 → 15 mm. Not the bottleneck.

The bottleneck is the water supply. Column water moves with u·∇W; the
water converging air brings with it (−W ∇·u) entered only as a 15 % share
(e17 `flux_convergence` 0.15) of the convergence smoothed over 300 km. The
full share lifts north India's July to 145 mm (224 observed) but floods
Mexico and eastern Australia: on a 300 km scale the drag step at every coast,
where onshore wind slows over land, counts as convergence. The monsoon
trough converges over 1,000 km and more.
